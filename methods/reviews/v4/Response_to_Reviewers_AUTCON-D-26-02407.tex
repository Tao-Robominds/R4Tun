\begin{responseblock}
Section~6 (Conclusions) now contains a dedicated limitations paragraph, separate from the future-work directions that follow. The limitations discussed are:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Single expert-tuned reference anchor:} all parameter adaptation is anchored to one expert-tuned configuration, which sets a low ceiling for both the LLM-guided and the rule-based methods. When the target tunnel is far from this anchor (e.g., the complex category), neither approach has a closely matching reference from which to recover strong performance.
  \item \textbf{Inherited SAM4Tun operator assumptions and failure modes:} R4Tun adapts, but does not replace, the underlying deterministic operators, in particular the fixed offset-and-order labelling template. Within these assumptions the LLM can only re-parameterise the pipeline, so the residual class-swap errors and the absolute-accuracy ceiling persist regardless of parameter choice.
\end{itemize}
\end{responseblock}

\section*{Reviewer 2}

\begin{commentblock}
I would like to commend the authors for their efforts in revising this manuscript. The authors have clearly taken the previous reviewers' comments. The expansion of the dataset to 30 tunnel subsets, the introduction of a deterministic non-LLM rule-based baseline, the rigorous cumulative ablation studies, and the inclusion of comprehensive statistical analyses have significantly elevated the methodological rigor of the paper. The concept of utilizing LLMs as white-box reasoning agents to adapt expert-designed pipelines via structured context is interesting.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
We thank the reviewer for this positive assessment and for highlighting the concept of our approach.
\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
However, despite the exhaustive experimental design, I have a fundamental and critical concern regarding the performance of the proposed system. The empirical results, as currently presented, fall drastically short of the standards required for practical deployment in construction automation, which severely undermines the core premise of the paper.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
Whilst we agree that the current performance levels are below those required for direct deployment in autonomous tunnel inspection, our objective is not to claim a deployment-ready solution, but rather to investigate a fundamentally different paradigm for tunnel segment point cloud segmentation: label-free adaptation through LLM-guided reasoning without re-training or tunnel-specific expert tuning. 

As the reviewer's detailed comments below all follow from this concern, we state our evidence-based position once here and refer back to it.
Three findings, from the new error analysis (Section~4.6) and the controlled comparison (Section~4.1), underpin this position:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The ceiling is a property of the fixed open-source SAM4Tun pipeline, not of the adaptation mechanism.} Even the open-sourced expert's tuning reaches only mIoU $= 0.531$ on its reference tunnel under our protocol; after adaptation false positives stay $\leq 6\%$ and detection is recovered, the residual error is dominated by class swaps from SAM4Tun's fixed detect-K-then-fixed-offset labelling rule, not by LLM parameter choices.
\item \emph{The mechanism itself is validated.} It produces statistically significant paired gains (Cohen's $d \approx 1.5$--$1.95$; 95\% CIs exclude zero), reaches the expert ceiling on regular tunnels label-free ($0.29 \rightarrow 0.50$--$0.54 \approx 0.531$), and the deterministic non-LLM baseline ($0.20$) shows the gain is not reproducible by rule lookup.
\item \emph{The scope is constrained accordingly.} R4Tun supports assisted, human-verified parameter adaptation, not autonomous final inspection or downstream tasks.
\end{enumerate}
We expand each specific point below.
\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
\textbf{Massive Misclassification:} An OA of $\sim$53\% implies that nearly half of the point cloud is incorrectly classified. An mIoU of $\sim$33\% indicates a drastic lack of overlap between the predicted segment masks and the ground truth.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
As noted above, the low figures have a structural cause. The error composition (Table~9) shows adaptation raises correctly classified points from 51\% to 71\% on regular tunnels (false negatives $21\% \rightarrow 2\%$) and recovers complex tunnels (false negatives $66\% \rightarrow 17\%$), with false positives $\leq 6\%$. 

The residual is class swaps ($\approx 21\%$ regular, $\approx 34\%$ complex); the complex swaps rise from $\approx 0\%$ only because adaptation recovers segments the baseline never detected and the fixed template then mislabels them. This also explains why mIoU trails OA: mIoU averages over classes and is dominated by the hardest minority segments (K, B2), and each swap depresses the IoU of two classes at once, so template swaps penalise mIoU far more than OA.

\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
\textbf{Failure in Downstream Tasks:} In the context of automated tunnel inspection (e.g., structural health monitoring, deformation analysis, water leakage detection, or Scan-to-BIM workflows), a segmentation pipeline with a $\sim$50\% error rate is practically unusable. The downstream geometric and structural analyses would be entirely compromised by the massive amount of noise, missing segments, and misidentified structural components.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
We agree autonomous downstream use is not supported at current accuracy, and the manuscript states this explicitly (Introduction, Discussion, Conclusion). We would, however, correct the error profile: the output is not dominated by noise or missing segments: false positives stay $\leq 6\%$ and false negatives fall to 2\% (regular) / 17\% (complex). The residual is class swaps from the fixed template. We therefore position R4Tun as an upstream, human-in-the-loop step (assisted parameter adaptation before expert review, and flagging tunnels far from the reference), with results feeding downstream tasks only after human verification.
\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
\textbf{The Low Baseline Fallacy:} The authors celebrate a $>$100\% relative improvement, but this is a classic statistical illusion caused by a catastrophically failing baseline. The static SAM4Tun baseline completely collapses on off-reference tunnels (overall mIoU = 0.15; complex tunnels = 0.04). Recovering from a state of total failure to a state of mediocre/poor performance (mIoU 0.34) does not validate the framework for real-world engineering applications.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
We have removed all celebratory relative-gain language; the Abstract, Discussion, and Conclusion now lead with absolute mIoU and OA. Importantly, our evidence does not rest on the relative gain: it is the paired, per-tunnel improvement, computed on absolute differences (not ratios) and therefore immune to low-baseline inflation, with large effect sizes (Cohen's $d \approx 1.5$--$1.95$; 95\% CIs exclude zero; Table~4). The 0.15 baseline is not an arbitrary comparator but the intended stress test: one fixed expert reference applied to 30 diverse tunnels. We present this as evidence for the adaptation mechanism, not for deployment readiness.

\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
\textbf{Poor Performance on Regular Tunnels:} Most concerningly, even on the regular tunnel subsets---which closely resemble the expert-tuned reference configuration---the mIoU only reaches $\sim$0.50--0.54. If an LLM, armed with explicit memory, intermediate state, and expert knowledge documents, cannot achieve a high degree of accuracy (e.g., mIoU $>$ 0.80) on in-distribution or near-reference data, it raises serious doubts about either the LLM's actual reasoning capability in this domain or the fundamental brittleness of the underlying SAM4Tun pipeline.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
This points to the pipeline, not to LLM reasoning. The expectation of mIoU $>$ 0.80 is not attainable from this pipeline: as noted above, open-sourced expert tuning itself reaches only 0.531 on its own reference under our protocol (the $>$0.90 figures are on curated, per-case-tuned rings). Against this ceiling, the LLM's 0.50--0.54 on regular tunnels is essentially the expert level, reached label-free and without per-tunnel tuning ($0.29 \rightarrow 0.50$--$0.54 \approx 0.531$). The LLM recovers detection (false negatives $21\% \rightarrow 2\%$) but cannot remove class swaps ($26\% \rightarrow 21\%$), which trace to the fixed labelling template rather than parameter choices. This is consistent with the non-LLM baseline not beating the static baseline here, whereas the LLM does.

\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
deployment of a system that misclassifies roughly 50\% of the structural point cloud? The authors must provide a realistic discussion on what specific, limited engineering tasks (if any) an mIoU of 0.34 can actually support, rather than broadly claiming it ``reduces manual per-tunnel re-tuning.''
\end{commentblock}

\begin{responseblock}
We have scoped, rather than removed, the re-tuning claim. The specific task supported today is \emph{assisted per-tunnel parameter re-configuration for regular (near-reference) tunnels under expert verification}: the system proposes a bounded parameter set with a logged rationale that the engineer reviews or overrides before use. The evidence is direct (above): on regular tunnels R4Tun reaches the expert-tuned ceiling label-free ($0.29 \rightarrow 0.50$--$0.54 \approx 0.531$) with no per-tunnel tuning, and the non-LLM baseline shows this is attributable to the LLM rather than to lookup. We frame this as reducing, not eliminating, expert effort, and note the benefit is presently bounded by the fixed labelling rule (Section~4.6); as that workflow improves (ring-level, dynamic labelling), the same mechanism is expected to extend the benefit to higher accuracy and a wider range of tunnels.
\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
\textbf{Q2. In-depth Error Analysis:} The manuscript currently lacks a qualitative and quantitative error analysis. Where are these massive errors concentrated? Are the LLMs failing to identify the correct boundaries? Is the 2D-to-3D reprojection introducing severe artifacts? Are background noises being classified as key blocks? The authors must provide visual error maps (e.g., False Positives vs.\ False Negatives) and analyze why the absolute metrics remain so stubbornly low despite LLM intervention.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
We agree. The new Section~4.6 adds FP/FN error maps for a regular (2-2) and complex (5-5) example (\MSErrorMapFigure), an error-composition table over all ground-truth points (\MSErrorCompositionTable), and a mechanism figure (\MSErrorMechanismFigure) naming the two structural constraints that bound mIoU. Answering the specific questions: 

(i)~the LLMs \emph{do} find boundaries: detection is recovered (false negatives $21\% \rightarrow 2\%$ regular, $66\% \rightarrow 17\%$ complex); the failure is in labelling located points, not finding them. 

(ii)~Reprojection is not the source: false positives stay $\leq 6\%$, so reprojection artefacts and noise-as-key-blocks are not dominant. 

(iii)~The errors are class swaps from the fixed labelling rule ($\approx 21\%$ regular, $\approx 34\%$ complex); the ring-to-ring K drift in \MSErrorMechanismFigure{} is the linking mechanism (sparse-ring density degrades K detection, then the fixed template rotates the whole ring's labels). The full constraint analysis is given in our response to Q4.
\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
\textbf{Q4. The Ceiling of the Pipeline:} The authors briefly mention the ``single-reference design'' as a limitation. However, if the underlying SAM4Tun pipeline is inherently incapable of achieving high mIoU due to its 3D-to-2D projection and SAM-prompting mechanics, then using an LLM to tune its parameters is merely ``rearranging deck chairs on the Titanic.'' The authors need to clarify whether the low mIoU is a failure of the LLM's parameter selection or a fundamental limitation of the SAM4Tun architecture itself.
\end{commentblock}
\begin{responseblock}
We thank the reviewer for this comment, which lets us locate the ceiling precisely. The reviewer hypothesises the limit lies in the 3D-to-2D projection and SAM-prompting; the error analysis indicates it does not. Were those the bottleneck, errors would appear as poor boundaries and spurious detections, yet false positives stay $\leq 6\%$ and detection is recovered (false negatives $21\% \rightarrow 2\%$ regular, $66\% \rightarrow 17\%$ complex). The ceiling lies instead in a deterministic labelling rule, bounded by two constraints not removable by tunnel-level tuning:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Per-ring density variation:} the per-ring point count varies up to $19\times$ (regular) / $38\times$ (complex) within a tunnel, so one tunnel-level configuration cannot keep both dense and sparse rings inside the detector's working band: a limit of adaptation \emph{scope}, liftable only at ring level.
\item \emph{Fixed segment-offset template:} segments are placed at hard-coded offsets from K in a fixed order, which fits regular tunnels but mislabels complex geometry even where points are correctly detected.
\end{enumerate}
That the ceiling is the pipeline, not the selector, is shown directly: expert tuning itself caps at 0.531. The low mIoU is therefore a property of the open-source \emph{implementation} (its tunnel-level scope and fixed template), not of the architecture or of LLM parameter selection. The adaptation is thus not ``rearranging deck chairs'': it produces real, significant movement toward the achievable ceiling, and the mechanism transfers to an improved pipeline. The concrete next step is replacing the fixed template with ring-level, dynamic labelling, which would raise the ceiling while the same mechanism operates.

\end{responseblock}

\end{responseblock}

\begin{commentblock}
